## Quick Summary

A bill of materials, or BOM, is a controlled list of everything required to build a specific project revision. A list containing only sensor, relay and wire is not enough for purchasing or troubleshooting. The BOM should identify each part, quantity, rating, function, source and alternatives that are genuinely compatible.

A good BOM helps students track design changes, manage spending and reduce the risk of late components. Prices vary by supplier and date, so record each price as a dated snapshot rather than a guaranteed project cost.

## Include fields that support rebuilding

Record a reference such as U1 or SENSOR1, description, manufacturer part number where available, quantity, critical specification, package or connector, voltage, supplier, date checked and approval status. For generic modules, preserve the listing, markings and photographs because the same product name may be sold with a different circuit later.

Separate required components, consumables, tools and optional upgrades. Enclosures, terminal blocks, cables and adapters are frequently forgotten even though they determine whether the final demo is reliable.

## Define critical specifications

An alternative is valid only when it meets the electrical and software parameters that matter. Two temperature sensors can differ in interface, range, response time, voltage and library support. For regulators, compare input range, output, current, heat and pinout. For relays, compare coil voltage, contact rating and actual isolation arrangement.

Mark parameters that must match and parameters that may vary. This turns purchasing into a defendable engineering decision.

## Control revisions and substitutions

Give the BOM a revision that matches the schematic and firmware. When a part changes, record the date, reason and effect on wiring or code. Do not overwrite the old list, because earlier test results may belong to a different hardware revision.

Create an as-built BOM when the prototype is complete. It records what is physically installed rather than what the proposal originally planned.

## Manage purchasing risk

Identify long-lead parts and buy them early only after interfaces have been checked. Keep limited spares for inexpensive failure-prone items, but avoid buying a full set before proving the highest-risk module. Consider seller traceability and counterfeit risk for critical devices.

Maintain a simple risk register containing part, possible issue, impact, mitigation and approved alternative. Typical risks include unavailable stock, a changed connector, incompatible logic voltage and abandoned software support.

## Validation Plan

- Check that every schematic block has a BOM item.
- Ensure identification is sufficient to purchase the same part again.
- Bench-test alternatives before marking them approved.
- Match the as-built BOM to the final prototype and wiring photographs.

Run tests with one controlled change at a time. Preserve configuration, raw measurements and failed runs rather than only successful screenshots. For a final year project, repeatable evidence is stronger than promotional claims. When the available instrument or method cannot prove a statement, report that limitation and define the additional experiment that would be required.

## Common Mistakes

- Using generic names without ratings or identifiers.
- Assuming replacement modules share a pinout.
- Presenting old prices as current commitments.
- Failing to record substitutions after testing.

Move these risks into a checklist or project risk register before demo day. A fault found while architecture and wiring are still flexible is normally less expensive to correct than one discovered after hardware, firmware and report chapters depend on the same assumption.

## FAQ

### Should software be included in a BOM?

A BOM normally focuses on hardware, but the report should keep a separate software and library inventory with versions. Both are required for reproducibility.

### Is a marketplace link enough to identify a module?

It helps, but listings can change. Preserve markings, specifications, photographs and the date alongside the link.
